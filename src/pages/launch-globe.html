Every launch, on one globe.

I love rockets. Few things beat watching a Falcon 9 booster come home and stick its landing, or an Electron climbing off the coast of New Zealand. This one is just for fun: every orbital launch attempt in history, {{lg_n}} of them from Sputnik in {{lg_first}} to {{lg_last}}, each lifting off its real launch site. Press play and watch the Space Race, the quiet years, and the launch boom we're living through right now.

While it plays, the globe turns toward wherever rockets are launching. Drag it to look around yourself, which pauses following; Follow launches or Reset view hands control back. Hover or tap a launch site for its count by provider. Choose providers above, or a year bar to jump there. Space plays or pauses. Busy years play slower, and when it gets crowded only some launches get an animated rocket (SpaceX and Rocket Lab always do). Flight paths are drawn for effect, not real trajectories.

The Soviet Union flew {{lg_ru_pk}} launches in {{lg_ru_pky}}, its busiest year, mostly from Plesetsk and Baikonur. Russia flew {{lg_ru_f}} in {{lg_full}}. All told, the USSR and Russia account for {{lg_ru_sh}} of every orbital launch ever.

Data and method

Where the numbers come from.

Launches
Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects (GCAT) launch list, as of {{lg_asof}}. I kept every launch GCAT codes as an orbital or deep-space attempt, successful or not, plus SpaceX's Starship flight tests. That's {{lg_n}} launches from {{lg_first}} to {{lg_last}}.
Providers
Grouped by rocket. "NASA" is Saturn, the Space Shuttle, and SLS, plus every other launch GCAT lists with NASA (or one of its centers, such as Goddard or Lewis) as the launch agency, like NASA's Thor-Delta, Atlas-Centaur, and Scout flights: {{lg_nasa}} in all. NASA missions flown on commercial rockets, such as crew and cargo flights on Falcon 9 or probes on Atlas V, count under the rocket's provider. "U.S. heritage" is the other Atlas, Thor/Delta, Titan, and Scout launches (military, commercial, and other non-NASA flights) before United Launch Alliance formed in late 2006. After that, Atlas V, Delta II, Delta IV, and Vulcan count as ULA. "USSR & Russia" includes Ukrainian-built Zenit, Tsiklon, and Dnepr, plus Sea Launch. Soyuz launches from Kourou count as Europe (Arianespace). CASC includes its Smart Dragon (Jielong) subsidiary. "China commercial" is CASIC's Kuaizhou and the private companies. "Other" covers Israel, South Korea, Brazil, and smaller U.S., Japanese, Indian, Australian, and European startups.
Starship
Starship flights are flight tests on suborbital or near-orbital paths, not full orbital launches. They count toward SpaceX's total here ({{lg_ss}} flights), and the globe labels each one as a test.
Left out
Suborbital and atmospheric flights (sounding rockets, missile tests, hop tests such as Grasshopper and early Starship prototypes, and Rocket Lab's suborbital HASTE flights, except one that GCAT codes as orbital), plus {{lg_drop_pad}} rockets that exploded on the pad before launch, such as Amos-6 in 2016, and {{lg_drop_abort}} countdowns where the engines lit and then shut down on the pad (five Space Shuttle aborts and Gemini 6).
Outcomes
GCAT's call on whether each flight reached its planned orbit or trajectory. Partial failures (the wrong orbit, or some payloads lost) are marked separately.
Booster landings
From Wikipedia's lists of Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches, matched to GCAT by launch time ({{lg_land_n}} Falcon flights, all matched). For Falcon Heavy, Wikipedia lists the center core only.
Sites and map
Pad and site coordinates come from GCAT's site tables. Air launches (such as Pegasus) start where the rocket was dropped. The land dots come from Natural Earth's 1:110m land outlines. Flight directions are rough, based on each site's usual launch corridor, and are for effect only.
Upcoming
The Space Devs' Launch Library 2 API, pulled when the site is built and refreshed in your browser when the copy is more than a few hours old. Launches with only a target month are listed without a day.

Data: GCAT (Jonathan McDowell, CC BY), Wikipedia (CC BY-SA), Natural Earth (public domain), The Space Devs. I built it with AI for fun, with reproducible Python build scripts. The globe is vanilla JS on a canvas, with no mapping library.
