Case study · Routing

Closer isn't always quicker.

A case study from my delivery and field-service routing work at a home-improvement retailer and a DVD kiosk rental company, rebuilt with public data: how traffic around Washington, DC and Richmond rises and falls through the week, and why the "nearest" stop depends on how you measure: a straight line, the road, or the clock.

The story

Miles aren't minutes.

At a home-improvement retailer and later a DVD kiosk rental company, I worked on delivery and routing optimization, and two ideas drove most of it.

Use real road distance, not a straight line. Straight-line (Haversine) distance is quick to compute, but a river or a missing bridge can turn a short hop into a long drive.

Add historical traffic. Being 20 miles closer doesn't always mean quicker once time of day and traffic are factored in.

At the retailer it was delivery, overwhelmingly appliance deliveries. The kiosk company ran far more than DVD kiosks: phone trade-in kiosks, trading-card vending machines, charging stations, and more. Merchandisers restocked the machines and service techs repaired or serviced them. Tech routing needed more work, because it factored in what each tech was working on. A screen replacement takes longer than a regular cleaning, and a long job pushes every later drive into a different hour of traffic. Try the service tech mode in the demo below to see it.

The same road-miles thinking later fed a bigger question at the kiosk company: which kiosks were worth keeping once you counted the drive to stock and service them. See the Keep or close dashboard.

    Beyond the map

    Why this matters for organizations.

    The same two ideas, real road distance and time of day, show up anywhere people or trucks have to be somewhere.

    01

    Delivery windows

    A promised two-hour window is only as good as the drive-time estimate behind it. Rush hour belongs in the plan, not in the excuse.

    02

    Field service

    Techs aren't interchangeable stops. A long repair pushes every later visit into a different hour of traffic, so the job type should shape the route.

    03

    Restock routes

    Merchandisers visit the same machines on a cycle. Small, repeatable savings per stop add up when the route runs every week.

    04

    Territory design

    Drawing service areas by straight-line radius splits neighborhoods that a river or highway already divides. Drive-time areas follow the roads.

    05

    Honest ETAs

    Customers notice when "15 minutes away" turns into 40. Time-of-day aware estimates set expectations people can trust.

    06

    Better questions

    Once distance and traffic are modeled well, you can ask the real question: not "who is closest?" but "who can get there soonest?"
